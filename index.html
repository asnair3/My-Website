EX09: Data Analysis for Continuous Improvement

Project Summary

The integrated analysis of the course survey data, processed through custom columnar and concat utilities, provides a data-driven foundation that supports the implementation of specialized conceptual walkthrough videos. By utilizing convert_columns_to_int to transform qualitative survey responses into a quantitative format, the resulting relational analysis reveals a clear positive correlation between perceived course difficulty and weekly time commitment. This suggests that students are not struggling due to a lack of individual effort, but rather due to the inherent complexity of the material. Because the categorical distribution of the data confirms a high density of students from diverse majors beyond computer science, the findings indicate that these individuals may lack the technical mental models required for rapid mastery.

Consequently, the data suggests that these students are already maximizing their "time on task" and would benefit more from targeted conceptual frameworks than from simply increasing their study hours. While the evidence supports the intervention, it also highlights the necessity of balancing new resources with existing workloads. By addressing the specific needs of this non-major population through structured visual aids, the course can better facilitate proficiency without exacerbating the high time demands already documented in the workload distribution.

Visualizations & Findings

1. Major Distribution

Finding: The categorical distribution of the data confirms a high density of students from diverse majors beyond computer science. This indicates that a large portion of the enrollment may lack the technical mental models required for rapid mastery, supporting the need for non-major focused interventions.

2. Weekly Study Hours

Finding: The workload histogram indicates a significant cluster of students spending over ten hours per week on coursework. This suggests that students who find the material most challenging are reaching a point of diminishing returns in terms of manual effort.

3. Difficulty vs. Time Spent

Finding: There is a clear positive correlation between perceived difficulty and weekly hours. This refutes the idea that students struggle due to lack of effort; instead, it proves that the most challenged students are often those working the hardest.

Final Conclusion

Through the combined analysis of the anonymized survey data from both instructors' sections, processed using the custom data utilities like concat and convert_columns_to_int. The categorical distribution confirms that COMP110 successfully reaches a diverse range of majors beyond Computer Science, this means that students come from various backgrounds with scheduling needs, technical comfort levels, and potential barriers to attending in-person office hours. The workload histogram reveals a significant number of students reporting 0-1 office hours visits per exercise, this means that the students who are currently struggling are not accessing current office hours which may suggest barrier issues like scheduling, transportation, commute or social anxiety. Lastly, by using the convert_columns_to_int we were able to process the survey responses which resulted in a scatter plot that shows high perceived difficulty are among those with low office hours usage, this shows that students are not struggling due to a lack in care but rather the complexity of the material itself. However, these students are not using the current in-person office hours. Some key-insights are the bottom-right quadrant of the scatter plot reveals an unmet need, students that are struggling the most are already maximizing effort but not receiving support through the current office hours. I would recommend implementing virtual office hours as a supplement to existing in-person office hours to address this unmet need from our findings.

Some potential costs are technology, TA time, training, and production cost. Virtual office hours will require a reliable Zoom license, screen-sharing capabilities, and recording storage. TAs will need additional hours for the virtual sessions alongside the existing in-person hours. There will also need to be training with the TAs so they can learn virtual help etiquette, screen-sharing practices, and managing remote questions. There will also be a high upfront cost for coordinating and scheduling the virtual sessions alongside the in-person ones. Some additional trade-offs include resource allocation, information overload, reduced in-person attendance, and technical issues. Resource allocation means that TAs will be spending more time on office hours which means less time for other tasks like grading. Information overload is when there are too many supplementary options to the workload and it may overwhelm students. There is a chance that students will stop feeling a need to come in-person for office hours if the hours are online so the attendance will decrease. There is also always a chance of running into technical issues that can cause problems with the TAs' time. Negatively impacted stakeholders include TAs, instructors and students with poor access to internet.

Some extensions and refinements include longitudinal tracking, barrier-specific surveys, comparative A/B testing, and virtual office hours optimization. Longitudinal tracking will help to identify exactly which unit spikes the highest in difficulty and need of virtual support, this will track students' difficulty ratings across the units. Barrier-specific surveys will help to identify which barriers virtual office hours will help address by asking questions and give options for scheduling, transportation, and anxiety. Comparative A/B testing will help provide causal evidence that virtual office hours supports students by testing the virtual office hours across different sections to measure if they lead to an increase in understanding. Lastly, virtual office hours time optimization will help ensure virtual office hour scheduling directly addresses the needs of the students with the highest difficulty rating by analyzing which time would work best for the students to have virtual office hours. To refine this idea in the future, the project should move toward longitudinal tracking to identify exactly which course units trigger the highest spike in difficulty, ensuring that virtual office hours directly address the most significant conceptual issues. Future work should also include a comparative A/B test across different sections to measure if virtual office hours actually lead to a measurable increase in "understanding" scores for high-difficulty students, ensuring that this instructional refinement addresses the most significant students' needs without increasing the overall time burden on students or staff.
